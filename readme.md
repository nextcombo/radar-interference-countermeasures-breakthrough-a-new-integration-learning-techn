In the complex electromagnetic environment of modern electronic warfare, radar, serving as the "eye in the sky" on the battlefield, faces increasingly severe interference threats. Especially with the advent of digital radio frequency storage (DRFM) technology, new types of phantom interference, with their realistic fake target simulation and low energy consumption characteristics, have become major challenges for radar systems. Accurate identification of radar interference signal types is the core premise for implementing effective countermeasure strategies, directly determining the initiative in electronic countermeasures.
Traditional methods of radar interference identification, whether based on likelihood estimation model matching or relying on expert experience for manual feature extraction, all have inherent shortcomings: the former requires extensive prior knowledge and high model complexity, limiting its applicability; the latter is time-consuming and labor-intensive, with the quality of feature extraction directly affecting recognition results, making it difficult to adapt to diverse new interference signals. With the rapid penetration of deep learning technology into the field of signal processing, convolutional neural networks (CNN), with their powerful automatic feature extraction capabilities, have opened a new path for radar interference identification, becoming a hot research topic in the field today.
Recent research papers focusing on CNN for radar interference identification, starting from two core issues of feature fusion and small sample learning, have proposed distinctive solutions, providing important technical references for practical radar anti-interference applications and showcasing the potential of deep learning in enhancing radar signal processing.
The core innovation of this study lies in the construction of a three-tier architecture that integrates 1D-CNN, 2D-CNN, and a fused network, achieving deep complementarity between time-domain and frequency-domain features of radar jamming signals. On one hand, by directly extracting the time-domain depth features of the original jamming signal with the 1D-CNN, the research team also inputs the real part, imaginary part, modulus, and phase of the signal into separate 1D-CNN sub-networks, then concatenates and fuses the extracted features to preserve as much of the original information in the time domain as possible; on the other hand, addressing the non-stationary nature of radar jamming signals, short-time Fourier transform (STFT) is employed to convert the raw signal into a time-frequency spectrum diagram. The 2D-CNN is used to extract deep features in both the time-frequency domain, capturing the changing patterns of signal frequencies over time, which is difficult to achieve with single-domain analysis alone.
On top of feature extraction, the research team designed a fully connected deep learning network for deep fusion of time-domain and time-frequency domain features. By using LeakyReLU activation function to alleviate gradient vanishing issues and combining batch normalization (BN) to accelerate model convergence, the final classification is achieved with softmax. To address the issue of deep learning models being prone to overfitting and lacking generalization ability, the study also proposed a Soft label smoothing (SLS) strategy. By introducing soft coefficients to optimize the probability distribution, the SLS reduces information loss while smoothing labels, further enhancing the model's robustness.

Even under different sampling rates, the model maintains high recognition accuracy, demonstrating good environmental adaptability. Classification errors mainly occur in interference mechanisms and signal types with similar parameters, fully verifying the effectiveness of multi-domain feature fusion.

On this basis, an ensemble model consisting of 15 structurally identical CNN subclassifiers is constructed, each corresponding to a subdataset with a specific feature combination. Unlike the traditional majority voting method, this study designed a weighted voting strategy based on the accuracy of the validation set, assigning greater weights to subclassifiers with higher recognition accuracy, effectively avoiding the misleading effects of low-accuracy subclassifiers on the overall results, allowing the ensemble model to fully aggregate the advantages of each subclassifier.
To address the issues of model training time consuming and lack of generalization ability under small sample conditions, the research team introduced model-based transfer learning: using the source model parameters trained based on the magnitude dataset as initialization parameters for other subclassifiers, only random initializing the first convolutional layer (adaptable to different feature channels), followed by a few iterations of fine-tuning to achieve optimal recognition results. This strategy allows the target model to share the learning knowledge of the source model, significantly reducing training time while significantly improving recognition accuracy in small sample scenarios.
The algorithm was validated on a dataset of 12 classes of deceptive interference, including both simulated and real-world data. Even in the extreme scenario of only 5% training samples, the overall recognition accuracy remained at 95.00%, significantly surpassing traditional 1D-CNN, 2D-CNN, and single CNN models. Additionally, the model's stability was notably superior to other methods.
